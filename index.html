About Me

I am a fourth-year PhD researcher in political science at the European University Institute, working on judicial behavior and the legitimacy of courts. My research asks what shapes judicial decisions and when citizens trust or question the justice system. I approach these questions through a gender lens, with particular attention to gender-based violence. I study how judges respond to public opinion and adapt to institutional reforms, and how the introduction of artificial intelligence into judicial processes affects decision-making and perceptions of fairness. My work combines administrative court data, causal inference methods, and experimental designs. I expect to complete my PhD in spring 2027.

Working Papers

  1. When Does Judicial Insulation Break Down? Public Pressure and Sexual Violence Rulings in Spain
    Using a high-profile sexual assault case as an exogenous shock and a difference-in-differences design on over 10,000 rulings, I show that conviction rates rise nationally only after the Supreme Court reversal, while judges in Navarra respond to concentrated local pressure.
  2. Specialized Courts, Unspecialized Institutions: Individual Learning and Organizational Stability in Gender Violence Adjudication (with Joan-Josep Vallbé) Under review, European Journal of Political Research.
    Exploiting the staggered introduction of specialised intimate partner violence courts in Spain (2005–2019), we find that district-level effects on convictions are modest and short-lived, while judges’ conviction rates rise with experience in these courts, consistent with individual learning rather than organisational change.
  3. Judge Ex Machina: Gender, Algorithmic Decision-Making, and Public Support for AI in Courts (with Siegfried Manschein)
    We compare LLM decisions with those of judges in Spanish sexual-violence cases, and use a conjoint experiment to test how AI involvement, transparency and judge gender shape public support for courts.
  4. Victim Identity and Judicial Outcomes during the Troubles
    Ongoing project, linking records of more than 3,500 conflict deaths during the Troubles (1969–2001) with court and archival sources to study how courts in a divided society treated violence against each community. Holding the perpetrator group constant, I examine whether the chances of a case being solved, prosecuted and punished depended on the victim’s community, and whether the introduction of the non-jury Diplock courts changed these patterns.
